# Memory cues in deep sleep

## What it does

Built in the phone app (2026-11-19) as the assistant's proposal, not yet tried on a head. Off unless you turn it on
in Settings. In the evening you learn where 32 objects sit on a board, half with a soft bell, half with a soft knock,
then test yourself. In the night's first hours, when the headband reads light or deep sleep, the phone plays one of
the two sounds very quietly. In the morning you place the objects again. Some nights are silent on purpose, and which
half's sound plays is hidden, so after 12 nights the app can test whether the sound did anything for you.

## Evidence for this headband: weak

The effect is small in the lab and several careful recent studies found none; a 13-lab replication is due around
2027. Reading light and deep sleep live from these four dry electrodes has not been checked against a sleep lab.

## What the research says

- The method is called targeted memory reactivation (TMR): something learned with a sound is replayed by that sound
  in NREM sleep (light sleep N2 or deep sleep N3). The one meta-analysis (91 experiments, about 2,000 people) found a
  small benefit, g = 0.29 (a fifth to a third of a standard deviation); after correcting for unpublished failures it
  is about 0.13 to 0.18. It worked in N2 (0.32) and deep sleep (0.27), not in dream sleep (REM) or awake. It showed in
  recall (finding the answer yourself), not in recognition (picking it from a list).
- A multi-lab replication of the classic sound-cue study (objects at places on a screen, the same kind of task as
  here; more than 13 labs, 600 people, preregistered) is running; its results were not public in November 2026.
- Several recent studies found no benefit at all: 40 words cued in N2 and 40 in deep sleep against 40 uncued gave no
  difference (Wick and Rasch 2023), and a nap study of object places could not repeat the effect (Cho 2025).
- At home, the best study with a headband (Dreem 2; 24 people, one night) played spoken words at 45 dB after 5 minutes
  of stable deep sleep, at least 9 s apart, pausing 30 s after movement or alpha waves: cued words were recalled 9.5
  percentage points better than uncued ones the next morning.
- A home study with only a wrist tracker found no effect overall; people who set the sound loud "almost always showed
  a reversed TMR effect". With lower volume limits, cued items beat uncued ones.
- Sounds that wake you hurt: in a nap study where half of the cues caused brief arousals, the cued places were
  forgotten more than uncued ones. In a home study with an mp3 player, people disturbed by the played words
  remembered worse.

## Where the data does not support it

- Every positive result used lab or headband sleep recording with frontal electrodes. This headband measures the back
  and sides of the head against a forehead reference, so deep-sleep waves from the front of the head should show on
  it, but no study has checked deep-sleep detection on this headband, and the live sleep model was never compared
  with a sleep lab here.
- One shared sound for half the board, rather than one sound for each object, is closer to the odour studies (one
  smell for everything learned) than to the classic study; it was chosen so a phone speaker plays only two quiet
  sounds all night.
- One person, 12 to 36 nights: with an effect the size of the best home study, about 24 nights with 20 objects a
  half would be needed to see it; 6 nights with sound can only show a large effect. Expect "not clear" and take it
  as an answer.
- Risks: sound in the night can disturb your sleep and a partner's, and sounds that wake you make the cued memories
  worse. No study tested nightly use for weeks.

## How the app uses this

- Off by default, in Settings, marked as the assistant's proposal with weak evidence. Never on a night with the
  lucid dream cues: one kind of cue a night (sounds mixed with other cues can cancel each other).
- Evening, about 12 minutes before Start night: 32 objects on a board, each shown at its place for 3 s with its
  half's sound; learning rounds (put it back, see where it was, hear its sound again; an object placed close leaves;
  at most 3 rounds); a test with no help and no sound; then, lying down with the phone where it stays, the loudness:
  each sound every 3 s from very quiet, 2 dB louder each time, until you tap "I hear it". The task counts for a night
  that starts within 3 hours.
- Night: from 30 minutes to 4.5 hours after Lights off; the live sleep model must read the two newest epochs (30 s
  each, read 2 minutes behind) as N2 or N3, each at least 0.6 sure; no awake or lost epoch in the 5 minutes before;
  no movement in the last 3 minutes and no jaw muscle in the last 30 s; the drift-off detector not saying awake. One
  sound every 10 s at most, 120 a night, 3 dB under the loudness you tapped and never louder. A stir after a sound
  (awake, or 3 seconds of movement, within 30 s) makes it 3 dB quieter and pauses 5 minutes; after 3 stirs, no more
  sounds that night. The recording and the smart alarm never depend on it.
- Morning: the same board, no help and no sound, and "did you hear a sound in the night?". The page shows the board
  with a line from each object's place to where you put it, and the numbers against the evening.
- The self-test: in each block of 4 nights 2 have sound; each night a coin picks the half whose sound plays; both
  drawn by the phone before the first night and locked with a fingerprint. A silent night does everything the same
  without sound. After 12 finished nights: on nights with sound, how much less the played half drifted overnight than
  the other half, tested against every way the coins could have fallen, with one verdict ("likely real for you", "not
  clear yet", or "too few nights" under 6 nights with sound and both tests). Hearing a sound, the other half's drift and
  stirs after cue moments are compared with the silent nights, without a verdict.
