# Lucid dream cues

## What it does

Built in the phone app (2026-11-19), not yet tried on a head. Off unless you turn it on. In the evening you practise
in bed for about 15 minutes: a soft cue of three rising tones, each time paired with "am I dreaming?". In the second
half of the night, when the headband reads dream sleep (REM) with the eyes moving, the phone plays the same cue
softly, hoping it reaches the dream and reminds you that you are dreaming. If it does, you can answer from inside
the dream with your eyes: left-right-left-right. Some nights are silent on purpose, so after 12 nights the app can
test whether the sound did anything for you.

## Evidence for this headband: weak

It worked in small sleep-lab studies with full sleep recording; the one controlled home test found no clear
difference between cues and no cues, a larger lab replication found none either, and reading dream sleep from these
four dry electrodes has not been checked.

## What the research says

- The method is called targeted lucidity reactivation (TLR). In the Swansea lab study (40 people, a morning nap after
  a normal night) a cue learned before sleep and played again in dream sleep was followed by a lucid dream proven by
  eye signals in 14 of 28 cued people against 2 of 12 uncued; the groups were not randomised between nap times.
- Two-way talk with dreamers is possible: in four labs, 36 people tried; some answered questions from inside a lucid
  dream with agreed eye movements or face twitches (Konkoly 2021). The answer signal is left-right-left-right,
  "large and at an even pace", easy to tell from ordinary dream-sleep eye movements (Konkoly 2026).
- A home phone app (Konkoly 2024) played the cue 6 hours after the evening practice, starting at the hearing threshold
  and slowly louder, pausing on movement. Without a control group, lucid dreams rose from 0.74 to 2.11 a week (19
  people). In the public test, nights with the trained cue were no different from nights with no cue at all; cues
  doubled movements in the night (0.19 against 0.10 per cue), and some users said the cues woke them like an alarm.
- A preregistered three-lab replication (60 people, two naps each; a preprint) found proven lucid dreams in 38 % of
  naps with cues against 28 % with sham cues: no difference. Cues were heard in 71 % of cued dream periods and
  disturbed sleep in 24 %.
- For comparison, without any device: in a study of 355 people, a memory technique with a short wake in the night
  gave a lucid dream on about 18-20 % of mornings against about 6 % before. Reviews call the method of intention the
  most effective; none found a technique that works reliably.

## Where the data does not support it

- Every positive result used lab sleep recording with eye and chin electrodes; this headband has neither. The live
  sleep model agreed with the full-night scoring on 89 % of epochs of one recorded night, but it was never compared
  with a sleep lab here, and dream sleep is the stage scoring without eye channels gets wrong most.
- The sideways eye movements the temple electrodes see come in every stage on this headband, deep sleep included, so
  they cannot mark dream sleep on their own; the app only asks for them as a second sign (with the back of the head
  quiet, which kept them in dream sleep more than in deep sleep on one night).
- The eye signal finder found no false signal in 6.5 hours of sleep of one night, but nobody has made a real signal
  on this headband yet, so whether it finds yours is unknown.
- Risks: sounds in the night disturb sleep (twice the movements per cue at home; disturbed sleep in a quarter of cued
  periods in the lab). Students who tried often to induce lucid dreams reported more sleep problems and, over two
  months, more dissociation (feeling detached from yourself or the world); experiments on harm are lacking. Experts
  advise against lucid dream work with psychosis.
- One person over a few weeks: the self-test will most likely say "not clear", which is an honest answer, not a
  failure.

## How the app uses this

- Off by default. The page says who should not use it: poor sleep, a sleep disorder, frequent nightmares, a history of
  psychosis or dissociation; and to stop if sleep gets worse or dreams and waking feel mixed up.
- Evening practice (the home app's): the loudness first (the cue every 3 s from very quiet, 2 dB louder each time; you
  tap when you hear it), then 4 cues with the words, 45 s apart, and 12 with eyes closed at 45-90 s gaps.
- Night: only from 4.5 hours after Lights off; the live sleep model must read the two newest epochs as dream sleep
  (each at least 0.6 sure), no deep sleep in the 10 minutes before, an eye burst in the last 2 minutes, no movement in
  the last minute and no jaw muscle in the last 15 s, and the drift-off detector not saying awake. One cue every 30 s
  at most, 10 in a dream period, 30 a night; the first at your practice loudness, 1 dB louder each time up to +6 dB. A
  stir or a wake after a cue makes it 3 dB quieter and pauses 5 minutes; after 3 stirs, no more cues that night.
- On the owner's recorded night (no sound was played) the rule would have given 30 cue moments: 28 in what the
  full-night scoring calls dream sleep, 2 in light sleep at the end of a dream period, none in deep sleep or awake.
- Morning: three questions (lucid? heard the sound? how much do you remember?), and a search of the night for
  left-right-left-right (3 even sweeps across) and, with a hands-free calibration, double jaw clenches.
- The self-test: in each block of 4 nights 2 have sound, drawn by the phone before the first night and locked with a
  fingerprint; a silent night does everything the same without sound. After 12 nights it compares lucid mornings
  after nights with sound and silent nights, shuffling labels inside each block; stirs after cue moments are compared
  too, so the silent nights show how much the sound itself wakes you.

## Libraries and models

- [AnySleep](https://github.com/dslaborg/AnySleep) (MIT): the sleep model read live, its three runs exported to ONNX
  for the phone.
- [flutter_onnxruntime](https://pub.dev/packages/flutter_onnxruntime) (MIT) with ONNX Runtime (MIT): runs the model
  inside the night recorder.
- Android's own media player plays the cue; no sound library.
- This project's own cue rule, eye bursts and dream signal finder (MIT); the Python references are
  `analysis/lucid_cues.py` and `analysis/lucid_signals.py`. Two open TLR projects were read for the protocol, not
  used as code: [TLR for iPhone and Apple Watch](https://github.com/rmallela26/TLR) (MIT) and
  [Dreamento](https://github.com/dreamento/dreamento) (MIT).
